Active TSP on Lines 5 & 6 Will be Hard to Implement Well

Transit signal priotity (TSP) is not a silver bullet. It will most likely improve travel times on Lines 5 and 6, but there is a limit to what it can do and riders may be disappointed if the City does not understand them. When you think of TSP, you probably think of active signals which will detect a vehicle and let it through. This page will focus on these active TSP systems, rather than passive systems, with a focus on their potential implementation on Lines 5 & 6 in Toronto. The ION LRT in Kitchener-Waterloo, which has some similar design elements and is not far from Toronto, is a good system to look at when looking at what challenges active TSP systems might face.

The ION LRT

The ION LRT is not perfect. Like Toronto's LRTs, it too suffers from signal delays and that has led the local advocacy group to call for better TSP. However, the line is faster than Toronto's system and we can learn from them. The ION's infrastructure is a good place to start. Even whenre it runs in the middle of the street, the streets it runs on are normally narrower than a Toronto arterial, normally two car lanes rather than four. The north end of the line is an exception, though it does not have any stations in that segment. These segments, despite being on-street just like Toronto's LRTs, feel faster than the street-running segments in Toronto.

The frequency is another aspect which will be important later. Lines 5 and 6 respectively run around every 4 and 6.5 minutes during peak hours, and every ~7 and ~10 minutes during the vast majority of off-peak hours. By contrast, the ION LRT runs every 10 minutes during the day on weekdays and every 15 minutes during most other times. This may be a downgrade for wait time, but it also makes TSP more reliable (as I'll discuss later on). These factors are both important when discussing the advantages the ION has when compared to Toronto's LRTs.

Why Lights Have to Stay Red

In this analysis, I am assuming car traffic flow is not considered at all. This is not how it is normally done, but it is important to establish an upper limit to TSP's effectiveness under current infrastructure. Times are taken from City policy or from equations I used in CIV332. I've been told the polcy is out of date and they may have become more conservative with signal timing (i.e. more time would be required) since this policy was published but I do not currently have evidence to suggest that is the case and my numbers have been accurate to what I have seen while riding Lines 5 & 6.

Amber and Red Signals

We can start by assuming left-turn phases do not exist. Not counting them, the last thing the intersection needs to do before letting the LRV go is stop traffic on the cross street. The City of Toronto mandates at least 5 seconds for this, though we also need to make sure drivers won't be forced to run a red light. We use the equation below to ensure we do not cause a dilemma zone, where a driver would be forced to run a red light due to being unable to brake on time. It additionally prevents drivers travelling at a reasonable speed from being unable to clear the intersection before the LRV gets a green light. \[t = \delta + \frac{u_0}{2a} + \frac{W + L}{u_0}\]

In this equation:

\(\delta\) is the reaction time (\(s\)), typically assumed to be 1 second.
\(u_0\) is the driver's speed (\(m/s\)), often assumed to be the speed limit.
\(a\) is the vehicle's acceleration as it slows to a stop (\(m/s^2\)). For cars, this is often assumed to be \(0.27g\), where \(g\) is the Earth's gravitational acceleration (\(9.81 m/s^2\)).
\(W + L\) is the width of the intersection and length of the vehicle, respectively (\(m\)). A car is typically assumed to be 6 metres long.

The first two terms of this equation ensure a driver is not forced to run a red light (minimum amber signal time). The third term ensures a driver continuing through the intersection has enough time to cross. Combined, these terms are the minimum allowed time for the amber ("yellow light") and all-red phases. When you use the speed limit term, divide the posted speed limit in km/h by 3.6, as 1 m/s = 3.6 km/h. The time is typically rounded up to the nearest second. With all our information and a roughly 40-metre intersection (this may vary slightly between intersections along Finch West), we find the amber and red signals need to be 7 seconds long on the cross street. If the intersection is greater than around 41.5 metres (I believe this would be measured from the front of the stop line to the end of the other side's crosswalk), it will need to be 8 seconds.

Pedestrians

Before the amber signal is the pedestrian coundown, referred to by traffic engineers as the Flashing Don't Walk (FDW). City policy requires this countdown to be as long as pedestrians walking at a 1.2 m/s speed to cross. There are additional rules, but they are not relevant to most intersections (and not the ones I looked at). Some places (e.g. Detroit) start the amber signal 3-4 seconds before the pedestrian countdown reaches 0, but I have never seen any jurisdiction in Ontario do this so I am assuming it would not be able to be implemented. Additionally, the walk phase must be at least 7 seconds long.

Dividing a 33-metre crosswalk length (measured via Google Earth at Islington) by the 1.2 m/s speed reveals the pedestrian countdown should be at least 28 seconds. While riding Line 6, intersections at which I was stopped early during a red phase (for the LRV) had pedestrian countdowns ranging from 27 to 32 seconds for most, and 35 seconds at Signet Arrow station, which is at intersection with a right-turn lane.

Inevitably, this leads us into a conversation about whether this is necessary. Because the LRT stations (excluding the termini) are at the centre of the road, any trip involving a surface station (which would be a majority of trips on Line 6) would require riders to cross the road before boarding or after alighting from an LRV. If pedestrian crossings were ignored entirely when an LRV approached, anyone boarding at that station would need to wait extra time to board their train. If the line is frequent enough such that trains regularly come to a station during multiple successive signal cycles (e.g. Line 5 during rush hour, possibly Line 6 during rush hours), pedestrians may need to wait a few minutes to access the station (unless they jaywalk). As I discuss further down, stations are the slowest intersections as LRVs need to stop immediately after the intersection. Additionally, around two-thirds of surface intersections on both lines have stations.

A two-stage crossing is an alternative which would allow pedestrians to continue accessing the station. However, at most stations on Line 6 this would only save 8-10 seconds as there is only one place for pedestrians to wait between cycles: The island with the station platform. At a far-side station, a pedestrian would have to cross three traffic lanes, a bike lane, and both LRT tracks in one phase but just two traffic lanes and a bike lane during the following phase. As a result, the time saved is closer to a third rather than half the time. This is still beneficial for reducing signal waits, but not as beneficial as one would hope. Additionally, it would force pedestrians crossing the road to wait an additional cycle to cross (crossing would probably take ~3 minutes). This may be a major problem if the intersection has high pedestrian volumes.

Some intersections along both Finch and Eglinton were not built with pedestrian refuge islands, preventing two-stage crossings from being implemented in those places. This infrastructure may be able to be built, but likely at the cost of more infrastructure construction.

The ION Advantage

This is where the narrower street the ION LRT normally runs on starts benefiting the line: While it also does not have refuge islands in much of its street-running sections (apart from where stations are placed), pedestrians have fewer lanes to cross. This allows for the crossing to be (in most cases) a bit shorter than a typical two-stage crossing along Line 6. Additionally, some sections of the line run on parallel streets reducing road width (and therefore pedestrian countdown lengths) further.

The ION additionally uses crossing gates on sections running off-street or on the side of the street. These would be possible to implement (with several seconds' notice) if pedestrians had places to wait on both sides of the tracks. Unfortunately, there are very few places on either Line 5 or 6 where this is possible (Eglinton Square near O'Connor station might be the only intersection where this is fully possible). Any additional infrastructure would require much construction including the widening of Eglinton Avenue at most intersections along the surface section. You may think this is worth the money, but it would be politically difficult.

Amber and All-Red Phases For the LRVs

Before the traffic signal turns red, the length of the amber and red phases must allow not only cars but also LRVs to cross the intersection. The equation below will apply again with some changes. \[t = \delta + \frac{u_0}{2a} + \frac{W + L}{u_0}\] The \(\frac{W + L}{u_0}\) term assumes the vehicle is going through the intersection at a constant speed. However, this is not the case at most intersections as two-thirds have a stop immediately adjacent to them. At a station I measured, this is around 9 metres. This varies between intersections (I remember another along Line 5 is 4 metres), but they are all very close to the intersection. We will need to use the kinematic equation from high-school physics to replace that term.

\[x = at^2 + ut + d\] We can assume the \(u\) and \(d\) terms are 0 as the LRV is stopped at the stop. This simplifies the equation to: \[x = at^2\]. The acceleration of the LRV is not the same as for a car. A spec sheet I found for a similar model from Alstom had a \(1.2 m/s^2\) "deceleration" (acceleration to a stop), but I have heard the TTC prefers to do this at \(0.9 m/s^2\) and the intersections I saw seemed to fit this number. This could be changed if the TTC would like, but passengers may need to get used to quicker acceleration (and hold on more). This assumes the line's overhead power could handle a greater acceleration (I don't know whether this is true). If it does not, replacing it would be costly.

We solve the equation at 2 displacement (\(x\)) values: 9 metres and 9 metres plus \(L+W\). This gives us the beginning and end of when the LRV occupies some portion of the intersection. We find the difference between these two numbers is 8 seconds for the higher deceleration value, and 12 seconds for the lower deceleration value at a typical intersection (when I was doing calculations initially, I used 43 metres as the length. It may be wider or narrower).

Adding the rest of the terms, we find that with the higher deceleration value, 16 seconds of combined amber and all-red time is still needed. With the lower value, it is 20 seconds. Intersections I have observed in practice have ranged from 17 to 22 seconds, though the lower values were at more minor intersections. On average, it was slightly lower than my calculations suggested (maybe a second).

The Total

Adding these numbers up, the signal needs 59 to 64 (probably 63-64) seconds of non-green time for the LRV in any cycle. It may be 8-10 seconds less if a two-stage crossing is possible. This means that to ensure a green light, the signal will need to have over a minute of notice in order to guarantee a green light for the LRV. If there's another train which activates an extended green or phase rotation (left-turn signal after the LRV's all-red), more time (at least 11, possibly 30 seconds) will need to be added. This would happen more often if the line runs very frequently, as if the line runs more often than every 8 minutes, LRVs will come on average during more than half of the two-minute signal cycle, during most of which they will need to activate the TSP.

More Complications

Due to the above complications, intersections are not considered predictable by traffic engineers. Unfortunately, the majority of intersections on Lines 5 and 6 are too close to each other (some much too close) to guarantee a green signal in this case. Thus, it will be very difficult to ensure LRVs get a green light with current infrastructure. This is in addition to current systems' inability to account for more than one LRV at once (the TTC's usual system is first-come, first-served: Either first vehicle to request TSP from the signal or first to reach the intersection).

Signal Coordination

This is the part where traffic flow starts to matter. Traffic signals are timed to allow drivers travelling in the peak travel direction to get a "green wave", going through successive intersections without hitting a red light if they are travelling at the speed limit. This can be used for cyclists or slower traffic speeds where that is applicable. I can sometimes get a green wave while running because of this. However, extended greens (which are often needed) can disrupt this cycle. To account for this, the cycle after a green extension is reserved to return the signal timing to its coordinated state. Because of this, you can not guarantee a TSP system with an extended green will activate more than once in a four-minute span without eliminating the signal coordination.

At that point, it may be better to instead coordinate the signals for transit vehicles, which would be more reliable if the system's non-intersection times can be predictable. This is called Passive TSP.

Passive TSP

This section is a work in progress and I am actively seeking review to ensure I am covering this topic as best as I can.

Passive transit signal priority is coordinating traffic signals to favour transit vehicles rather than other modes. Signals would be timed for expected transit vehicle travel times including stopping, rather than those of other modes. For something like this, you would still need stops to be mostly reliable to allow the LRVs to run on a set schedule. However, since the signal timing is built into the TSP implementation you do not need to worry about getting the signal back to coordination. The TSP would thus be able to work with highly frequent services. However, it still has a limit.

The Limit

To ensure the signals work the same for all successive trains, the signal cycle length will need to be a factor of the headways on the line. This is harder but not impossible for highly frequent lines.

Signal cycles in Toronto tend to be around 2 minutes, and Toronto’s pedestrian guidelines will likely mandate a cycle length nearing that value. If we assume a cross street is similarly wide but does not have an LRT line, it will require around 12 fewer seconds for its part of the cycle. I use this value to ensure consistency: The signal coordination must factor in the widest street it crosses with the longest necessary pedestrian countdowns.

As mentioned further up, LRVs need to have a red light for 63 seconds without a two-stage crossing, but that includes the cross street’s 7-second amber and all-red. However, there are some roads with right-turn lanes that take longer like Signet Arrow on line 6 (which has a 35-second pedestrian countdown).

Factoring that intersection in, we find the cross street needs 49 seconds and the LRV’s street needs around 55 seconds. You will also need to include left turns which take another 11 seconds each. That totals 2 minutes and 6 seconds. If two-stage crossings are implemented, this is reduced to around 1 minute and 55 seconds. Two times this value is 3 minutes and 50 seconds, which is already right around how often line 5 runs right now.

Line 5’s rush hour frequency already approaches this number, as it is currently running at 3:47 headways in the morning and 3:56 headways in the afternoon. Lines 5 and 6 should not run any more frequently than this, unless they specifically decide to run it every two minutes though this might cause more problems as I will discuss later. If Line 5 needs a major increase in capacity, it will probably be more effective to add a third car to the existing tram couples, as improved frequency will be harder to coordinate with.

Having six and a half minute headways makes it a bit easier for Line 6. At a 7-minute headway, you can choose a 1:40 cycle or a 2:20 cycle and both would be reasonable. With headways a bit less than that, a slightly over two-minute cycle would work and be similar to the existing signals.

You might ask if the system would have major problems in the event of a delay. If a train holds too long at a station and gets a red, it should only at most be delayed by the length of a signal cycle. Since the system would be designed for a green wave, the vehicle would continue as normal after it hits its first red.

Signal Timing

Signal timing gets complicated in three separate ways. Firstly, any change in service frequency over time, such as after the end of rush hour, would complicate timing. This can be mitigated by using a headway that is also a multiple of the pre-existing cycle length, but it may limit your options for determining an appropriate headway.

Service changes (particularly changing the line’s headways) would require a new signal timing plan as the cycle length would need to change to accommodate the new headways. This may make service changes require more time and money to implement than they would otherwise. Adding a third tram to the existing coupled LRVs will also cause a change in signal timing as it would need more time to cross the intersection, but this change could be done while keeping the existing cycle length, especially if the green time used for the LRVs is not the bare minimum.

Additionally, you might not even be able to have the time you need to change the signal timing. Any sufficiently long slow zone would need to be accompanied by changes in signal timing. These may be difficult to plan for as slow zones are normally implemented for safety reasons rather than being planned for ahead of time. Signal timing changes may thus need to happen faster than the City is accustomed to.

What About the Other Direction?

Within above parts of the Passive TSP section of this page, I have not accounted for the fact that LRVs come in both directions. Coordinating traffic signals for traffic in both directions is very difficult, so difficult in fact that traffic engineers only coordinate for regular traffic in a peak direction. This is fine for lines where there is a clear peak direction. However, the counter-peak direction will not enjoy any TSP and intsead it’ll be left to random chance. This could be mitigated by implementing active TSP only in the opposite direction, though if your line is too frequent it’ll be very difficult to do and it will be impossible if your cycle length is near the bare minimum.

The Takeaway

Transit Signal Priority is not a silver bullet. Especially on wide roads, pedestrians' needs much be taken into account whether through infrastructure or signalling which does not give the LRVs a green when the pedestrians have not finished crossing their tracks.

Passive TSP has a lot of promise. When implemented well, it can prove reliable in areas where active TSP has trouble. However, it comes with a lot of difficulties which may doom it especially if your transit line does not have a clear peak direction. This kind of surface transit will always be subjected to surface traffic no matter where it is. If all this sounds complicated and you just want a reliable way to give transit the green light, perhaps the line needs to be grade-separated or crossing gates and necessary associated infrastructure.
